How to film a dispersal order at a protest: what to capture

One of the most disputed moments afterwards, and one of the most often missed.

When police order a crowd to disperse, what happens next is argued over for months: whether an order was really given, whether anyone could hear it, whether there was a way out, how long people had. Most footage misses it, because everyone is filming something else. This page is about capturing that moment safely, and about leaving when you are told to.

Short answer. When police order a crowd to disperse, capture whether an order was actually given and by whom, whether it was audible where the crowd stood, whether it named a way out and whether that way was open, and how long passed before force was used. Film from the edge, say the time aloud when the order is given and again when the line moves, then leave. In France, staying after dispersal orders can itself be an offence (article 431-4 of the Code pénal). Witness Camera uploads every 15 seconds, so what you filmed survives a seized phone.

What to capture

  • That an order was given, and by whom. Film the person giving it, if you can do so from a safe distance, and anything that identifies who they are.
  • Whether it was audible. Film from where the crowd is standing, pointed towards the source, so the recording captures what could actually be heard at that spot.
  • Whether it named a way out, and whether that way was open. Pan slowly along the route that was announced.
  • The time. Say the time aloud when the order is given and again when the line moves. The gap between the two is what is later disputed. If you worry that your voice would identify you, stay silent and let the time show on the recording instead.
  • The lead-up. The twenty calm minutes before establish that a march was peaceful and where everyone stood.

Then leave

Filming does not protect you from the consequences of staying. In France, voluntarily remaining in a gathering after the dispersal orders is an offence (article 431-4 of the Code pénal): one year in prison and a €15,000 fine, raised to three years and €45,000 if the face is deliberately concealed, according to the LDH legal note and secondary summaries of the text. Lawyers note that the offence requires the staying to be voluntary, which can be contested if someone did not hear the orders, but that is a defence to raise afterwards, not a plan. The rules differ by country and we have not verified them for the others. Check them for where you are, and when in doubt, go.

Stay safe while you film

  • Keep two exits in mind and recheck them when the crowd moves. The real danger is being enclosed.
  • Do not move towards the line to get a better shot. Footage from the edge is more useful and safer.
  • Hold the phone visibly and horizontally, and do not use digital zoom. Both are advice from Index, a French digital-investigation NGO.
  • Do not physically obstruct anything. Obstruction, not the camera, is the real legal risk in the countries our guides cover.

If you are in Germany

Witness always records sound and has no setting to turn the microphone off. German law treats recording the non-publicly spoken word as an offence (section 201 StGB), and whether an officer's announcement at a public operation counts is disputed. See our German legal page. If you want to work without sound, Witness is currently the wrong tool.

Why upload while recording

A dispersal order is exactly when phones get taken. Witness sends a piece every 15 seconds to storage you own, hashes each piece and time-stamps the session, so the footage of the order is likely to be safe before anyone reaches you. If the queue falls behind on a congested network, it sends a small low-resolution copy of each new piece first. Nothing is deleted from the phone until your storage confirms receipt.

Questions

What should I film when police order a crowd to disperse?

Whether an order was given and by whom, whether it was audible where the crowd stood, whether it named a way out and whether that way was open, and the time when the order was given and when the line moved. Film from the edge and leave when told to.

Is staying after a dispersal order illegal?

In France, voluntarily remaining in a gathering after the dispersal orders is an offence under article 431-4 of the Code pénal, with higher penalties if the face is concealed. The rules differ by country and we have not verified them for others. This is not legal advice.

Can I keep filming after the order is given?

Do not assume filming protects you. If you stay, you may be committing an offence whether or not you are filming. Comply with the order to leave, and film as you go if it is safe to do so.

How does Witness help here?

It uploads a piece every 15 seconds to your own cloud storage, so the footage of the order is likely to have left the phone before it can be taken. It records with the screen black, hashes every piece and time-stamps the session.
