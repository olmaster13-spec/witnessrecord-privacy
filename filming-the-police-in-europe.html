Filming the police in Europe

There is no single answer. The law is set country by country.

Ask whether it is legal to film the police in the United States, and the honest answer has one shape: every federal circuit that has squarely considered it has recognised a First Amendment right to record officers performing their duties in public. Ask the same question about Europe, and there is no equivalent single answer. There is no European court ruling that settles it, and the countries covered on this site take genuinely different positions.

This page explains what is shared across the continent, then links to a dedicated guide for each country, written in that country's own language, based on that country's own law.

What is shared

Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights protects freedom of expression, including the freedom to receive and impart information, and the European Court of Human Rights has recognised a strong public interest in the transparency of police power. In a 2023 ruling, Bild GmbH & Co. KG v. Germany, the Court found that ordering a news outlet to stop publishing unedited images of a police arrest violated Article 10. That case is about publishing existing footage, not about the initial act of a bystander filming, and it should not be read as settling the broader question. It shows the general direction of the Court's reasoning without closing the gap between countries.

GDPR sits underneath all of this as a second, EU-wide layer, and it answers a different question than the one people usually ask. It does not generally decide whether you may record. It governs what counts as processing someone's personal data once a person is identifiable in your footage, which mostly becomes relevant at the point of publishing or storing footage rather than at the point of pressing record. The exemption for purely personal activity stops applying the moment you post publicly to an unlimited audience. The journalistic exemption is narrow and national, but the Court of Justice of the European Union held in Buivids (case C-345/17, 2019) that it can cover a private citizen who is not a professional journalist, if the purpose is genuinely to disclose information to the public rather than something else.

Neither of these gives a country-by-country answer on its own. What follows does.

Country by country

CountryFilmingThe distinctive risk
France Generally protected. An attempt to restrict it was struck down. Physical obstruction, not filming itself.
Germany Video without audio is generally allowed. Recording audio can be a separate, more serious offence.
Italy No specific law forbids it. Publishing, if it compromises an operation or is defamatory.
Spain Constitutionally protected, without needing authorisation. Distributing images that provably endanger an officer or operation.
Brazil Protected by the Constitution. Officers who block filming can face charges themselves. Publishing footage intended to humiliate, outside the evidence exception.
Poland Recording needs no consent. Publishing without blurring, following a real 2024 court ruling on personal rights.
Netherlands Generally protected as public oversight. Publishing recognisable footage without blurring, under officers' portrait right.
Romania Generally protected on public property. Sharing images showing an officer's face, badge number or vehicle.
Greece Recording is broadly protected by case law. Publishing is genuinely unsettled and assessed case by case.
Sweden Constitutionally protected to record and to publish. Defamation, if publication is meant to show contempt.

Why country pages, not one translated page

The tempting shortcut is to write one article and translate it four times. That would be dishonest here, because the law is not the same underlying fact wearing four languages. Spain has a specific statute addressing distribution of police images. Germany treats audio recording as meaningfully riskier than video. France had a real attempt at restriction that its own constitutional court struck down. Italy has no specific statute either way and relies on general principles. A translation would flatten those into one false average.

So each country page was researched and written for that country, in its own language, with its own sources. Where two country pages happen to say similar things, it is because the law happens to be similar, not because one was copied into the other.

Where this site covers the United States instead

If you are in the US, the country-specific material above does not apply to you, and the site's original guides are built around US law: how to film the police, step by step, and whether police can take your phone or delete your video, which works through Riley v. California and the seizure-versus-search distinction that Europe does not share in the same form.

What this page cannot do

None of this is legal advice, in any country. I am a developer, not a lawyer in any of these jurisdictions, and I read primary and secondary sources carefully while building this. That is a different thing from advice you can rely on in a specific situation.

Ten countries are covered so far. Not because the rest of the world is settled or simple, and Brazil is not even in Europe, but because getting each one right, with real sources, takes real time, and a page claiming to cover a country I have not actually researched would be worse than not covering it at all. Countries whose current legal or political situation would need fundamentally different guidance, rather than a translated version of this one, are deliberately not here yet.

Law changes. Each country page carries the date it was written. Check it is still current before relying on it.
