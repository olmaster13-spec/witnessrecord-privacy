How to sync videos from multiple phones

Audio, a clap, a marker or a shared clock: what works, what fails, and how to shoot for it.

Two phones filmed the same moment and now you have two files that start at different times. Lining them up is easy when you planned for it and slow when you did not. This guide covers the four methods in use, what the main editors offer, and what to do on the day so the edit takes minutes. Written by the developer of Witness Camera, so read it with that in mind; where another approach is better, it says so.

Short answer. Most editors sync phone clips by matching the sound, so keep audio on for every phone and make a loud clap or call out the time at the start. If the sound is poor, line clips up by a marker you set at the same moment. Phones rarely share timecode, so the other option is to stamp every recording on one common clock before filming, which is what Witness Crew does on iPhone.

Why clips from different phones do not line up

Each phone starts recording when its owner presses the button, so every file begins at a different moment. Each phone also keeps its own clock, and two phones that both show the same minute can still disagree by a second or more. A file's start time is therefore good for putting clips in rough order and poor for lining them up to a frame. See why phone clocks disagree for how far off they can be.

Method 1: sync by audio waveform

This is the default in most editors. The software compares the sound in each clip and slides them until the waveforms match. Apple's documentation says to record audio on every camera and recording device in a multicam production, and that Final Cut Pro's automatic angle synchronization relies on audio waveforms. Apple also says some recordings are not suited to the feature and that long processing times are possible.

  • Works best when every phone hears the same loud event: a clap, a shout, a siren, a crowd chant.
  • Fails when one phone is muted, wind covers the microphone, the phones are far apart so the sound arrives at different times, or the only sound is quiet and continuous.
  • Sound travels slowly. A phone 30 metres from the clap hears it about a tenth of a second after a phone beside it, roughly three frames at 30 frames per second. Close enough for most edits, visible in a tight cut.

Method 2: sync by a clap or marker you set yourself

If automatic sync gives up, find one sharp moment that every clip shows, such as the clap, a flash, a door slamming or a car passing, and mark it at the same point in each clip. Final Cut Pro can use the first marker of each clip as the sync point. Frame.io's tutorials describe setting a marker at the same moment in each clip and choosing markers for angle sync in DaVinci Resolve. It takes a minute per clip and works whenever the moment is visible or audible in every one.

Method 3: sync by timecode

Cinema cameras and audio recorders can share a timecode signal, and Premiere Pro and DaVinci Resolve can sync by it. Phones do not normally share timecode with each other, so this method is for dedicated cameras. Rode's guide for Premiere Pro describes the Timecode choice in the Synchronize Point menu for gear that has a timecode generator.

Method 4: sync by a shared clock set before you film

If all the phones agree on the time before recording starts, each file's start time can be used to place it on the timeline with no audio matching at all. This is useful when the audio is unusable, for example a loud crowd, wind or a phone filming in a pocket. Witness Crew does this on iPhone: the phones measure their clocks against each other, every recording is stamped with its offset, and the app can export timelines with every angle already placed. See Witness Crew and how to film an event with several phones.

Shooting so that syncing is easy

  • Keep sound on for every phone, including the one filming from a distance.
  • Start every phone before the action, and make one loud, sharp sound where all of them can hear it.
  • Do not stop and restart a phone mid-event if you can avoid it. Every restart is a new file to sync.
  • Overlap: if one phone must stop, another should still be running, so you never have to bridge a gap.
  • Note the time and place each phone started, in writing, straight afterwards.

A note on evidence

Syncing is for watching and editing. If the footage may matter as evidence, keep every original untouched and work on copies, because editing changes a file's fingerprint. Hashing and time-stamping explains why, and the verification page checks a Witness recording in your browser without uploading it.

Questions

How do I sync videos from different phones?

Import all the clips into a video editor and sync them by audio, which Final Cut Pro, Premiere Pro and DaVinci Resolve can all do. If the sound is poor, mark the same moment in each clip, or place them from start times recorded on a common clock.

Can I sync phone videos without a clap?

Often yes, if the phones recorded the same loud sound such as a crowd, a siren or music. A clap or spoken count makes it reliable, and a marker or shared clock covers the cases where the audio does not match.

Why do my synced angles drift apart?

Common causes are a clip with variable timing, a long clip synced at only one point, or phones far apart so the sound arrives at different moments. Check the start and the end, and split and re-sync long clips.

Do phones share timecode?

Not normally. Timecode sync is meant for cameras and recorders with a timecode generator. For phones, use audio, a marker, or a common clock.

Is there a free way to record several phones on one clock?

Witness Crew on iPhone is free. The phones are matched to the crew organizer's clock and each recording is stamped with its offset. It is not available on Android yet.
