Police told you to delete your video or hand over your phone at a protest

A calm sequence for the moment, what the law says in five European countries, and what happens to footage that has already left the phone.

This page is for the specific moment at a European demonstration when an officer tells you to stop recording, to delete what you have, or to hand over the phone. For the United States, see Can police take your phone or delete your video?. Rules differ by country and by circumstance. Nothing here is legal advice.

Short answer. If an officer at a protest in Europe tells you to stop filming, delete a video or hand over your phone: stay calm, do not obstruct, say you are filming in a public place, ask for the legal basis of any seizure, note the officer's number and the time, and take legal advice afterwards. In Italy and Germany, our legal pages say a verbal order to delete, with no judicial order, has no legal basis. Footage that Witness Camera already uploaded is in your own cloud storage, not on the phone.

Do not make it a physical argument

In every country our guides cover, the real limit is obstruction, not the camera. Standing at a reasonable distance with the phone visible is generally protected. Physically getting in the way, or refusing a lawful order to move, is not, whether or not you are filming. Arguing the law at the roadside rarely helps and can turn a calm moment into an arrest.

Say it once, calmly

  • "I am filming in a public place."
  • If asked to delete: you can say politely that you do not agree to delete anything. Whether an officer can then seize the phone is a separate question that depends on the country and the circumstances.
  • If the phone is being taken: ask for the legal basis, and ask for the officer's identification number.
  • Then stop talking about the law. Your remedies come afterwards, with a lawyer.

What the law says, country by country

These are the short versions from our legal pages, which give the sources and what we could not verify.

  • Italy. A verbal order to stop recording or delete images, without a seizure order from the judicial authority, has no legal basis. Remaining calm and declining without physical opposition is the firmest position. Italian legal page.
  • Germany. A verbal request to delete the recording has no legal basis without a court or prosecutor's order. Sound is the separate risk, under section 201 StGB. German legal page.
  • France. Filming officers on duty in public is protected expression. In 2021 the Constitutional Council struck down a specific offence on publishing identifiable images of officers. An order given on the ground, even without a legal basis, should be handled calmly. French legal page.
  • Spain. The Constitutional Court held in 2020 that capturing images of officers on duty needs no prior authorisation. Using them in a way that creates a real, proven danger can be an administrative infringement under article 36.23 of Organic Law 4/2015. Spanish legal page.
  • Greece. The Areios Pagos has held that recording police conduct on duty is not within the officer's private life. Publication is weighed case by case. Greek legal page.

Passcodes and unlocking

Whether you can be required to unlock a phone differs by country and situation, and we have not verified it for the countries above. Ask a lawyer before you need to. What you can do in advance is use a six-digit passcode instead of Face ID or a fingerprint, as the protest phone checklist recommends.

What to remember, and write down at once

  • The officer's identification number or name, if visible. In France, officers on duty have worn an individual identification number (the RIO) since 2014.
  • The time, the place, what was said, and who else saw it.
  • Write it down as soon as you can. Memory degrades quickly under stress.

Where your footage is

Witness uploads a piece every 15 seconds to the storage account you connected, hashes each piece and time-stamps the session. Log in to Google Drive, Dropbox, Nextcloud or your WebDAV host from another device to see what arrived. Your emergency contact receives each recording as it is made. Anything that had not finished uploading was still on the phone. The hashes and the time-stamp can be checked by anyone with OpenSSL, without the app.

Afterwards

  • Contact a lawyer or a legal-support organisation before you publish or hand over anything.
  • Do not edit the original. Work on a copy if you want to blur faces.
  • Blur minors and bystanders before posting online.

Questions

Can police in Europe make me delete a video?

It depends on the country and the situation. Our Italian and German legal pages say a verbal order to delete, with no order from the judicial authority, has no legal basis. For France, Spain and Greece our pages advise handling an order on the ground calmly and asserting your rights afterwards with a lawyer. None of this is legal advice.

Can police take my phone at a protest?

In some circumstances, such as a seizure under the relevant procedure, they can. Ask for the legal basis and do not resist physically. Whether and when you must unlock the phone differs by country and we have not verified it.

Is my footage lost if my phone is seized?

Not what had already uploaded. Witness sends a piece every 15 seconds to your own storage, so that footage is safe. The piece being recorded and any queued pieces are on the phone.

Should I argue that I have a right to film?

Say once that you are filming in a public place, then stop. Debating the law on the spot rarely helps. Your remedies come afterwards, with a lawyer.
