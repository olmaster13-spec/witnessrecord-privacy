Is it legal to film police in France?

An English guide for visitors, expats and reporters at the 2026 protests.

France has had four straight weekends of mass protests, and many of the people filming are not French. This page answers the question in English and says what we are sure of and what we are not. Written by the developer of Witness Camera, so read it with that in mind; where another approach is better, it says so.

Short answer. Filming on-duty police in a public place is generally not an offence in France, and the Conseil constitutionnel struck down in 2021 a provision that would have punished malicious publication of police images. The real risks at a protest are different: staying in a gathering after dispersal orders, obstructing officers, and publishing images with an intent to harm. Police cannot lawfully demand that you delete footage, but they can seize a phone as part of a criminal procedure. This is general information, not legal advice. Laws change and courts differ; check with a local lawyer or a legal-observer group before you rely on it.

What is happening

Protests that began in September over living costs and conditions in schools have continued for several weeks. CNN reported the largest day of protests in years on 5 November, with tear gas used in Lyon, and CNBC reported clashes on a fourth straight weekend on 7 and 8 November. Further dates are announced by unions and student groups at short notice, so check current news the day before and treat any date in this page as out of date.

What the law says, as far as we can verify

  • Filming police in public. There is no general offence of filming on-duty police in a public place. A 2021 attempt to criminalise the "provocation to identify" officers with a manifest intent to harm them, in the "sécurité globale" law, was struck down by the Conseil constitutionnel (decision 2021-817 DC of 20 May 2021) as too imprecise. The government had said the target was publication with intent to harm, not filming.
  • Blurring officers' faces. A secondary legal guide states that blurring is not a legal requirement for publishing since that decision. We could not check this against the primary text, so treat it as unconfirmed.
  • Staying after a dispersal order. Remaining in a gathering after being told to disperse can be an offence in its own right, and the penalty is higher if the face is concealed. Filming does not protect you from this. Our French guide to filming a dispersal order explains the signals.
  • Publishing violent images. French law punishes some publication of violent acts, with exceptions for evidence and for professional news activity. See the French legal page for the detail and the articles to read in the original.
  • Deletion and seizure. Police have no power to order you to delete footage. A phone can be seized as evidence in a criminal procedure, which is one reason to upload while recording.

We could not retrieve the primary texts from Légifrance while preparing this page. Penalties and article numbers in French law change; read the current text or ask a French lawyer before relying on them.

If you are a visitor or an expat

  • Nothing in French law that we know of gives foreigners fewer rights to film than French citizens, but a foreigner arrested at a protest faces extra consequences, such as immigration and visa questions. Ask your embassy or a lawyer if you are unsure.
  • Keep your passport or residence card with you if you are required to carry it, and keep your phone locked.
  • Stay out of areas the police have closed. Paris authorities have banned some gatherings and refused requests; a banned gathering is not a safe place to film.

Practical steps in France

  1. Set up the phone before you leave. Use our French checklist or the English one.
  2. Film from a distance, behind the line, with a wide frame. Say the time and place aloud only if you choose to.
  3. If asked to leave, leave. If asked to delete, say politely that you will not, and show your hands.
  4. If you are injured or witness an injury, keep the footage and the time. In France you can report police conduct to the IGPN, the national police's internal inspectorate, or to the Défenseur des droits. An IGPN online report is not the same as a formal complaint, so ask a lawyer how to file one. See after the protest: who to give the footage to (in French).

Questions

Is it legal to film police in France?

Filming on-duty police in a public place is generally not an offence. The risks at a protest come from other rules: staying after a dispersal order, obstructing officers, and publishing images with an intent to harm.

Can French police make me delete my video?

They have no power to order deletion. They can, in a criminal procedure, seize a phone as evidence, which is why uploading footage off the phone while you record is sensible.

Can I post a video of police on social media in France?

Generally yes, with care. The Conseil constitutionnel struck down a 2021 provision on malicious publication of police images, but other rules on privacy and on violent images still apply. Consider blurring bystanders and take legal advice for sensitive footage.

Is it safe to film French protests as a tourist?

The risk is mostly from being close to clashes, tear gas and dispersal operations. Stay out of banned or closed areas, keep a distance and leave when told to.

Where can I complain about police behaviour in France?

The IGPN inspectorate and the Défenseur des droits are two routes. An online IGPN report is not a formal complaint; ask a lawyer how to proceed.
