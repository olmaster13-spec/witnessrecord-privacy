Gen Z protests: how to document them safely with your phone

One set of habits that works in any country, plus where the law differs.

Young people have led protests in dozens of countries in the last two years, and almost every one of them has produced phone video of police. This page is for anyone holding the phone: a student, a parent, a reporter or a bystander. The habits below hold everywhere. The law does not, so each section says where to check. Written by the developer of Witness Camera, so read it with that in mind; where another approach is better, it says so.

Short answer. Decide what you are for (evidence, news or testimony), film wide and steady from a safe distance, keep the phone's contents safe from seizure by uploading while you record, never promise yourself that deleting on demand is required, and write down times and places straight afterwards. The legal position on filming police differs by country, and filming never makes you immune from a dispersal order. Witness is a free iPhone and Android app that records with the screen dark and uploads footage as it is recorded, but it cannot hide the recording indicator and it always records sound.

Where the protests are

Coverage by CNN, CIVICUS, Context and Britannica describes youth-led protest waves across Europe (France, Germany, Spain, Portugal, Italy, Greece, Belgium, Albania, Bulgaria, Serbia, Turkey), Asia (Nepal, Indonesia, the Philippines, India, Bangladesh), Africa (Kenya, Madagascar, Morocco, Tanzania, Togo) and Latin America (Peru, Mexico). Lists differ between sources and the situation changes weekly, so treat this as a map of where the habits below are most needed, not a ranking.

In early November 2026 the heaviest day-to-day coverage is from France, where CNN reported the largest day of protests in years on 5 November and CNBC described clashes on a fourth straight weekend on 8 November. Organisers of the German "Gen Z regelt" protests told the Washington Post that more than 100,000 people took part over the Halloween weekend. Figures from organisers and governments rarely match; use them only as a rough scale.

Before you go: five habits that work in any country

  1. Decide your role. Evidence for yourself, material for a news outlet and a record for a lawyer need different things. Our guide to what to do with the footage covers who to give it to.
  2. Set up the phone at home. Update it, charge it, turn on a passcode, switch off previews of messages on the lock screen, and test your recording app once. A panic is the wrong time to find a permission prompt. The protest phone checklist has the full list.
  3. Send footage off the phone while you record. Phones get dropped, confiscated and unlocked under pressure. Footage that is only on the phone is the footage you are most likely to lose. Witness uploads a 15 second piece at a time to storage you own, such as Google Drive, Dropbox or Nextcloud, so most of a recording is elsewhere by the time you stop.
  4. Agree a plan with someone. Tell a trusted person where you will be and when you will check in. Witness can send a text with your last known position to an emergency contact when you tap Send.
  5. Think about other people in the frame before you post. Footage you keep as evidence and footage you publish are different decisions. Faces of bystanders, minors and anyone who could be targeted later are the reason to hold back, blur or ask first. See how to anonymise people in protest video.

During: what to film and how

  • Film wide, steady and long. A single wide shot that shows what led up to an incident is worth more than a close, shaky clip that starts after it. Hold the phone horizontally.
  • Keep your distance and stay behind any line the police set. Filming does not give you the right to cross a cordon, and obstruction is the usual reason people are stopped while filming.
  • Know the dispersal signals. If police order a crowd to leave, staying can itself be an offence in many countries, however peaceful you are. Our guides cover filming a dispersal order and filming an arrest.
  • Say the time and place aloud, if you choose. It helps the timeline. It is also on the soundtrack, and some guides advise against speaking at all. Both are fair choices; pick one deliberately.
  • If you are told to stop or to delete, stay calm and keep your hands visible. Do not argue about the law at the scene. See can police make you delete your video? and what to say if asked.

After: the first hour matters most

  • Write down the time, place, units and anything the footage does not show while you still remember it.
  • Do not edit the original. Work on copies. Edits and re-exports change a file's fingerprint and make it harder to show it is unchanged.
  • Give the original to a person who can use it: a lawyer, a legal-observer group, a news desk or a complaints body. See Witness for legal observers and the verification page, which checks a Witness recording in your browser without uploading it.

The law depends on where you stand

In many democracies filming on-duty police in public is lawful, with limits on obstruction, on recording audio and on publishing identifiable officers. In others it is risky or criminalised. Do not assume the rule from a country you know. Guides we have written so far:

If you are filming in a place with a history of violent policing or of prosecuting people who film, speak to a local lawyer or a local human-rights group before you go, and consider whether the risk of being seen to film is higher than the value of the footage.

Questions

How do I film a protest safely?

Film wide and steady from a distance, stay behind any police line, upload footage off your phone while you record, tell someone where you are, and write down times and places afterwards. Know whether staying after a dispersal order is an offence where you are.

Is it legal to film police at a protest?

In many democracies it is lawful to film on-duty police in a public place, with limits on obstruction, audio recording and publishing identifiable officers. In others it is risky or an offence. The rule depends on the country you are standing in, so check before you go.

What if the police take my phone?

They may be able to seize it in some circumstances, and a passcode and encryption on the phone help. Footage that has already uploaded to your own cloud storage stays out of their reach. Whether you can be made to unlock it depends on the country, so do not hand over a passcode at the scene and ask for a lawyer.

Which app records a protest and uploads while recording?

Witness Camera for iPhone and Android uploads a 15 second piece at a time to Google Drive, Dropbox, Nextcloud or WebDAV while recording. Other tools, such as Tella and ProofMode, take different approaches; see our comparison.

Can I film with the screen off?

Yes, with Witness the display stays dark while it records. The iPhone green camera dot and orange microphone dot, and the Android notification, cannot be hidden by any app.
