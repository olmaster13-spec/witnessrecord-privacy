Is it legal to film police in Germany?

Filming is generally allowed. Publishing and audio are where people get into trouble.

Germany saw large student-led protests at the end of October, and many people filming them are asking this in English. The honest answer has three parts: filming, publishing and sound. Written by the developer of Witness Camera, so read it with that in mind; where another approach is better, it says so.

Short answer. Filming police operations in Germany is generally permitted. The risk lies in publishing identifiable footage without consent, which can breach the Kunsturhebergesetz (up to one year in prison or a fine), and in recording audio, which is a separate issue under section 201 of the Strafgesetzbuch. Police may ask filmers to identify themselves. Courts disagree on details, so ask a lawyer about footage you plan to publish. This is general information, not legal advice. Laws change and courts differ; check with a local lawyer or a legal-observer group before you rely on it.

Filming

German legal commentary says that filming and photographing police operations is generally permitted. The criminal exposure under the Kunsturhebergesetz (KUG) arises when images are distributed or shown publicly without the consent of the person depicted. Police may still ask you to identify yourself. One older administrative court decision reported by Legal Tribune Online (LTO) found that police could not simply assume that footage would be published; they needed concrete indications. That decision is about 13 years old, so check whether it still reflects the case law.

Publishing

  • Portraits of police officers generally cannot be published without their consent, unless the publication relates to a police operation of special public interest, according to German legal guides.
  • Breaching the right to one's own image under section 33 KUG can carry up to a year in prison or a fine. A Berlin Kammergericht ruling in November 2023 dealt with a fine for filming a police operation.
  • One lawyer's guide advises blurring faces when publishing videos of routine police operations. Footage kept as evidence and given to a lawyer or court is treated differently from footage posted on social media.

Sound

Recording the non-public spoken word without consent can be an offence under section 201 of the Strafgesetzbuch. How this applies to officers on duty in public is contested, and we do not claim to settle it. Witness always records sound, and you cannot turn it off. If you are in Germany, that is a real consideration; ask a lawyer, and think about who is speaking close to your phone.

What to do in practice

  1. Film wide, from a distance, behind the line.
  2. Keep the original. If you publish, blur identifiable faces of officers and bystanders first.
  3. If police ask for your identity, comply with identification; do not give a passcode. Ask what the legal basis is and note the time.
  4. Give evidence footage to a lawyer or a recognised legal-observer organisation rather than posting it first. In German see Polizei filmen: was ist erlaubt? and the guide for school and student protests.
