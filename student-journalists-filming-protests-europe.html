Student and campus journalists: filming protests safely and lawfully

The same limits as anyone, a few differences, and a publishing workflow that protects you and the people on camera.

Student reporters are often the ones with the best access to a school or university protest. They face the same limits as everyone else on the street, plus a few questions of their own: whether a journalism exception applies, how to verify what they filmed, and how not to expose the people in it. This page is a practical guide, with the legal points taken from our country pages.

Short answer. Student and campus reporters filming protests have the same legal limits as anyone, with exceptions that may or may not cover them. In France the offence of recording and spreading images of violence does not apply to the normal exercise of a profession informing the public or to footage made as evidence. Italy's privacy code has a news-reporting exception that does not require a professional card. Greece applies a case-by-case journalism exemption even to non-professionals. Whether a campus outlet counts is not settled in what we checked, so do not assume it. Keep originals, verify, blur minors and have a second person review before publishing.

The legal points that differ by country

  • France. Recording and spreading images of violence is an offence in itself (article 222-33-3 of the Code pénal, five years and €75,000 for spreading them), but the text does not apply to the normal exercise of a profession whose purpose is to inform the public, or to a recording made to serve as evidence in court. Whether an unpaid student outlet counts as such a profession is not clear from what we read, so ask a lawyer.
  • Italy. The privacy code has an exception for news reporting: sharing an image to communicate news of general interest, without sensationalism or violating dignity. You do not need to be a professional journalist. Minors need particular caution. Italian legal page (in Italian).
  • Germany. Publishing identifiable images without consent can infringe the right to one's own image (sections 22 and 33 KUG). There is an exception for events of contemporary historical interest, but its scope is disputed. Sound is a separate risk under section 201 StGB. German legal page (in German).
  • Greece. The Court of Justice of the EU (case C-345/17, Buivids) held that citizens recording and publishing for a journalistic purpose can fall within the data-protection exemption, even if they are not professionals, assessed case by case, including whether blurring was available. Greek legal page (in Greek).
  • Spain. Filming officers on duty needs no prior authorisation. Using the images in a way that creates a real and proven danger can be an administrative infringement under article 36.23 of Organic Law 4/2015, with respect for the right to information. Spanish legal page (in Spanish).

A safe workflow

  1. Record with provenance. Witness hashes every piece, writes a manifest and time-stamps the session, so you can show the footage is unaltered and dates from when you say.
  2. Never edit the original. Work on a copy for cutting and blurring.
  3. Check what metadata travels with the file. Witness can record your location in each recording's chain-of-custody record if you allow it. Decide whether that metadata should go out with a published copy.
  4. Blur minors and bystanders on the copy, and think about whether a face is needed at all.
  5. Get a second person to review before publishing: an editor, a lecturer, or a lawyer for anything involving violence or minors.
  6. Do not stream live. Index advises against broadcasting live, and a private upload to your own storage, which is what Witness does, is not a stream.

Protecting people and sources

Your footage can identify protesters as well as officers. Keep it in storage you control, do not share raw files in group chats, and do not name or tag people. In Italy, the new decree-law 190/2026, in force from 23 October 2026, allows arrests within 48 hours based on video for some offences committed in demonstrations, which makes the handling of raw footage more sensitive still.

Verification

Anyone can check Witness footage with OpenSSL, without the app, by comparing each file's hash against the manifest and checking the time-stamp token. See how to verify footage. A hash shows a file is unchanged since recording. It does not show that the footage is a fair picture of events.

Questions

Do student journalists have a legal exception for filming protests?

Sometimes, and it is not settled for campus outlets. France exempts the normal exercise of a profession informing the public and footage made as evidence. Italy's news-reporting exception does not require a professional card. Greece applies a case-by-case journalism exemption. Ask a lawyer before relying on any of them.

How do I show my protest footage is authentic?

Record with an app that hashes each piece and time-stamps the session, never edit the original, and keep the manifest and time-stamp proof. Witness does this, and the checks can be run with OpenSSL.

Should I blur faces before publishing protest footage?

Yes for minors and bystanders, on a copy, keeping the original. A face is rarely needed to tell the story.

Is it safe to livestream a protest?

Index advises against filming live. Witness does not stream: it uploads private pieces to your own storage.
