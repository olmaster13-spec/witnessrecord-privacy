Is it legal to film police in the UK? A guide for England and Wales

You may film. Police still have other powers.

People searching this are usually standing at a protest or have just seen an arrest. The short version is reassuring and incomplete. This guide covers England and Wales; Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate laws. Written by the developer of Witness Camera, so read it with that in mind; where another approach is better, it says so.

Short answer. In England and Wales there is no offence of filming or photographing police in a public place, and Liberty says you may film police, including at a stop and search or arrest. The Public Order Act 2023 stops police using certain powers for the sole purpose of preventing someone observing or reporting on a protest. Police need another reason, such as obstruction, to act against you, and they have no legal power to make you delete footage. Publishing or sharing images of officers can carry separate risks. This is general information, not legal advice. Laws change and courts differ; check with a local lawyer or a legal-observer group before you rely on it.

What the guidance from rights groups says

  • Filming is lawful. Liberty states that you are legally allowed to film or photograph police, including when observing or intervening in a stop and search or arrest, or when monitoring and reporting on their conduct at a protest.
  • Police need another reason to stop you. Liberty adds that police must have some other lawful reason to use their powers against someone monitoring a protest, such as obstruction. The Public Order Act 2023, as Liberty describes it, bars police from using their powers for the sole purpose of preventing someone from observing or reporting on a protest.
  • No power to delete. Guides from rights organisations say officers have no legal power to delete your images or destroy film. A phone can be seized in some circumstances, such as a lawful arrest; ask for a solicitor.
  • Publishing carries its own risk. Privacy International's fact sheet warns that there are circumstances in which it may be an offence to publish or distribute a photograph of a police officer, and notes that photos posted online can be used to identify people at a protest. It refers to section 58A of the Terrorism Act 2000, which concerns collecting or publishing information about police that is likely to be useful to someone preparing an act of terrorism. A reasonable-excuse defence exists. Read the fact sheet and take advice if your footage identifies officers in an unusual way.

Where it can go wrong

  • Obstruction. Standing in the way of officers, blocking a line or interfering with an arrest can be treated as obstruction, whether or not you are filming.
  • Conditions on protests. Police can impose conditions on assemblies and processions under the Public Order Act 1986. Ignoring them is an offence, and filming does not excuse it.
  • Police buildings and sensitive sites. Filming near police stations and some other sites can raise security concerns and attract questions.
  • Being filmed back. Police also film protests. If privacy matters to you, think about what you post and who is identifiable.

If an officer tells you to stop or to delete

  1. Stay calm and keep your hands visible.
  2. Ask which power they are using and why. You do not have to argue the law at the scene.
  3. You may keep recording unless there is a lawful reason to stop. Do not unlock your phone or give a passcode at the scene; ask for a solicitor if you are arrested.
  4. Write down the time, place and any officer numbers straight afterwards.

Where to get help

Liberty publishes advice for people monitoring and reporting on protests, and legal observer groups attend many demonstrations. See Witness for legal observers. The detail of any one case belongs with a solicitor who handles protest and public-order law.

Questions

Is it legal to film police in the UK?

In England and Wales there is no offence of filming police in a public place, according to guidance from Liberty. Police can still act on other grounds, such as obstruction. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate laws.

Can UK police make me delete my footage?

Rights organisations say police have no legal power to make you delete images. A phone can be seized in some circumstances, such as a lawful arrest.

Can I post a video of police officers online in the UK?

Often yes, but Privacy International warns that publishing photographs of officers can in some circumstances be an offence, including under section 58A of the Terrorism Act 2000. Get advice if the footage is unusual.

Does the Public Order Act 2023 protect people filming protests?

Liberty says it prevents police using certain powers for the sole purpose of stopping someone observing or reporting on a protest. They can still act if there is another lawful reason.

Can police film me at a protest?

Yes, police do film protests. Think about who is identifiable in anything you post.
