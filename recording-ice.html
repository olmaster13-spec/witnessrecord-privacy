Recording immigration enforcement

Documenting an ICE encounter, and making sure the recording outlives it.

Recording federal immigration agents carrying out their duties in public is constitutionally protected activity. The practical difficulty is rarely whether you may record. It is whether the recording still exists afterwards.

This page is not legal advice, and it is not a know-your-rights guide. For that, go to the organisations that write them: the Immigrant Legal Resource Center, the National Immigrant Justice Center, and United We Dream. What follows is about the recording itself.

What the standard guidance says

Broadly consistent across the organisations above, and worth reading in full from them rather than summarised here:

  • Keep your distance. Rapid response guidance commonly suggests recording from at least ten feet, and complying immediately if agents instruct you to move back.
  • Stay where you have a right to be. Public space, or property you have permission to be on. Recording does not create a right of entry.
  • Do not physically interfere. Obstruction is the charge that most reliably follows, and it is the one thing filming cannot protect you from.
  • Capture the details a video misses. Agent names and badge numbers, vehicle plates, the time, the exact location, how many people were taken and where they said they were going.
  • An administrative warrant is not a judicial warrant. Guidance from these organisations notes that ICE forms I-200 and I-205 are signed by an agency official rather than a judge, and do not by themselves authorise entry into a home.

The advice everyone gives, and its weak point

Nearly every guide includes a version of the same instruction: make sure your video is syncing to the cloud, so that if something happens to the phone, someone else can still reach the footage.

That instruction is right, and the mechanism usually suggested for it is weaker than people assume. iCloud Photos and Google Photos are built for convenience, not for this. Both routinely defer uploads until the phone is on Wi-Fi, charging, or above a battery threshold, and both generally wait for a recording to finish before it becomes eligible to sync at all. A ten-minute video is one file, and that file does not begin uploading until you stop recording.

So the gap is precise: the footage most worth having is the footage recorded in the minutes before a phone is taken, and that is exactly the footage still sitting on the device.

Closing that gap

The fix is to stop treating a recording as one file that uploads at the end. Witness cuts continuously into roughly 15-second pieces, finalises each one the moment it ends, and uploads it while you are still filming. A phone taken at minute nine has already sent minutes one through eight to storage you own.

Other properties that matter in this specific situation:

  • The screen stays black. The phone reads as idle rather than as a camera pointed at agents. It can sit in a pocket or a hand at your side.
  • It starts without unlocking. The iPhone Action Button, a Lock Screen widget, or a Siri phrase. Raids do not wait for a passcode.
  • Someone else gets it automatically. Nominate a rapid response coordinator, an organiser or a lawyer once, and each recording's folder is shared with them as footage arrives, without you doing anything in the moment.
  • Nothing passes through the developer. There is no server and no copy, so there is nothing to subpoena from us. Footage goes from the phone to your own cloud account and nowhere else.

Set it up before the day. An app that has not been connected to a storage account saves nothing. This is the step people skip and the one that decides whether any of the rest matters. The field guide covers it in five minutes.

Why an unedited record matters here

Footage of enforcement activity is routinely disputed, and the dispute is increasingly not about what happened but about whether the video is genuine. Witness hashes every piece with SHA-256 on the device before it moves, lists those hashes in a manifest with capture times and coordinates, and has an independent time-stamp authority counter-sign the manifest.

The result is checkable by a lawyer, a newsroom or a court without trusting you or us: the video hashes to the manifest, the manifest hashes to the signed token, and the token came from a third party with no stake in it. The verification steps are written for someone who has never used the app.

One practical warning: sending footage through a messaging app or posting it to a social platform re-compresses the file and breaks the hash match. Share originals, or a link to them.

What this does not do

It cannot upload without a network. Inside a building, a basement or a facility, pieces queue on the device until there is signal.

It cannot protect you from being compelled to unlock the phone. Uploaded footage is beyond the reach of the device, but not beyond the reach of the cloud account the phone is signed into.

It does not make anything lawful that is not. It gives no legal advice and no protection from an obstruction charge.
